## (b) Notes and citations

### 1. Weather, December 1740 – March 1741

**1.1 Invasion march, 16–31 December 1740 (Lower Silesia, Crossen to Glogau to Breslau)**
- Reddaway, *Frederick the Great and the Rise of Prussia* (1904), Gutenberg #56387, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/56387/56387-h/56387-h.htm. SNIPPET: "Frederick's worst foe, indeed, was the weather, which tested the endurance of the Prussians and found it great. Torrents of rain fell from the eighteenth of December to the twentieth."
- Carlyle, *History of Friedrich II*, vol. XII, Gutenberg #2112, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2112/2112-h/2112-h.htm. SNIPPETS:
  - "Rain in plunges, fallen and falling, through that blessed day; making roads into mere rivers of mud."
  - "Rain ending, there ensued a ringing frost."
  - The search summary places Frederick at Milkau on 19 Dec "just come in out of the rain". It also says Carlyle calls the frost unfavourable for siege work at Glogau. That last point is REPORTED only.
- Ranke, *Geschichtsbilder* (projekt-gutenberg.org, ch. 43). SNIPPET: Frederick arrived before the first fortress (Glogau) in December "nach einigen starken und wegen der Witterung nicht eben bequemen Märschen".
- Warfare History Network (Hawkins/Blumberg). REPORTED: the advance on Breslau was "exceedingly slow due to the snow and mud". This is popular history.
- **Assessment.** Three independent secondary narratives agree on heavy rain and mud on the march in the third week of December, then frost. **INFERRED baseline:** late Dec 1740 in Lower Silesia was wet, with deep mud on unmade roads, then turned to frost before the year's end.
- These are not meteorological records. The primary basis is probably Frederick's letters of 16–31 Dec 1740 (PC vol. 1, and the *Œuvres* letters to Jordan). The Generalstab volume, *Die Kriege Friedrichs des Großen* I.1 (1890), narrates the march day by day. Neither was opened.

**1.2 Background climate 1739–41**
- Brönnimann et al., "The weather of 1740, the coldest year in central Europe in 600 years", *Clim. Past* 20, 2219–2235 (2024), https://cp.copernicus.org/articles/20/2219/2024/. REPORTED from search summaries:
  - In the ModE-RA reconstruction, 1740 is the coldest year back to 1421.
  - The coldest 12 months ran November 1739 to October 1740.
  - Frost waves came in Nov 1739, Jan 1740 (the most extreme, with Scandinavian blocking) and Feb–Mar 1740.
  - Jul–Aug 1740 were cold and rainy and the harvest was late. The summary quotes: "the harvest, delayed by a cold and wet August, took place in difficult conditions", and the fruit harvest was good.
  - The year was "again cold in October" 1740.
  - The paper's data are on figshare, DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.25879186. It may hold station series near Silesia; the summary mentions Hanov at Danzig for Jan 1740. Not checked.
- The paper's focus ends before December 1740. It does not answer the December question directly.
- EGU General Assembly 2018 abstract, https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018EGUGA..20.2636Z/abstract. SNIPPET: the cold 1739/40 winter "recurred in the 1740/1741 winter in the observations".
- A second, unnamed article (REPORTED) says the cold snap "was followed by two further cold winters in 1741 and 1742".
- A Czech geography paper on extreme winters (geografie.cz, geo_2010115030266.pdf). REPORTED: the 1740 extreme cold is clearest in north-western European series, and the Czech documentary data do not single it out so strongly. This is a caution against carrying the Dutch and English picture straight onto Bohemia.
- **Brázdil on 1740 in the Czech Lands specifically: NOT FOUND** as a dedicated paper.

**1.3 Spring 1741**
- amtsberg-wetter.de, "Klimahistorie" (a Saxon local site citing Glaser, *Klimageschichte Mitteleuropas*, p. 73). REPORTED: winter 1740/41 had much snow and great cold, then a thaw with ice drift and flooding in March 1741, and the winter ended only on 13 May.
- **DISPUTED:** a follow-up search could not confirm whether this entry belongs to 1740/41 or to 1739/40. In 1740 the cold also lasted into May and June. Check Glaser p. 73 before using it.
- Mollwitz, 10 April 1741:
  - Snow and fog on the morning of the battle are widely REPORTED (en.wikipedia "Battle of Mollwitz").
  - The "two feet" of snow comes from Asprey via a blog, and from the Royal Collection Trust map description. A wargaming blog (olicanalad.blogspot.com) disputes the depth. **DISPUTED.**
  - Wikipedia says Neipperg camped at Neisse "in atrocious weather". REPORTED.
  - Neipperg crossed from Moravia via Zuckmantel and Ziegenhals in late March and early April 1741. REPORTED.

### 2. Provisioning (Austrian and Prussian, c. 1740)

**2.1 Bread**
- Prussian: *Setting Nutritional Standards*, ch. 4, "Setting standards: the soldier's food in Germany, 1850–1960" (Cambridge), https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/setting-nutritional-standards/setting-standards-the-soldiers-food-in-germany-18501960/BB3F4CB72C0BC3D9362F5B306B73BB15. SNIPPET: "Friedrich's soldiers were supplied with just two pounds of bread per day, and food procurement and preparation were largely left to the individual soldier."
  - This is a modern secondary source. It does not say which Friedrich year.
  - 2 Prussian lb × 467.7 g ≈ 0.94 kg (INFERRED).
- Kabinettskriege blog, 2017, "What did an average eighteenth-century soldier eat?". REPORTED: Prussian bread was stopped from pay in peacetime and issued free in war; meat 2 lb a week. Blog-level only.
- Perjés, "Army provisioning, logistics and strategy in the second half of the 17th century", *Acta Historica Acad. Sci. Hung.* 16 (1970). SNIPPET: "The daily bread ration per man was about 1 kg in all European armies."
- Austrian, 1740: **NOT FOUND.**
  - Out-of-period (1792–97) study on academia.edu, https://www.academia.edu/5666865/. SNIPPET: "Brotportion entsprach 1,75 Pfund gutem Mehl, trocken und voll ausgebacken". Do not import this into 1740 without a 1740s source.
  - A blog citing Duffy gives Austrian 1.75 lb free. REPORTED, and probably the same later regulation.
- **INFERRED working range** for the adjudicator, explicitly provisional: 1.75–2 local pounds of bread a man a day, about 0.9–1.1 kg, for both armies.

**2.2 Forage (the horse "Ration")**
- **NOT FOUND for 1740**, Austrian or Prussian.
- Out of period:
  - Austrian 1792–97 oats portion of 1/8 Metzen (SNIPPET, same study), about 7.7 L of oats (INFERRED from U3).
  - A 19th-c. Hanoverian ration (light: 10 lb oats, 8 lb hay, 5 lb straw; heavy: 12/10/5) in *Militär-Wochenblatt* (REPORTED). Not usable for 1740.

**2.3 Wagon load, bread carried, field bakeries**
- **NOT FOUND.** Searches returned only general claims:
  - Frederick's "mobile iron ovens" (blog, REPORTED).
  - French field ovens "about three days' march from the grain stores" (Lower Rhine, REPORTED, not Austrian or Prussian).
- Perjés 1970 (SNIPPET): the main problem was collecting the stocks and processing them "by milling and baking".
- Do not let the adjudicator use the "five-march" magazine-to-oven rule as fact for 1740.

**2.4 The governing Austrian regulation (the likely source of P3–P6, C11)**
- *Militar-Verpflegs-, Disciplins-, Bequartierungs-, Marche-, Vorspanns-, Recrout- und Rimontirungs-Regulament für die gesammte in denen Kaiserl. Königl. Teutschen Erb-Landen verlegte Miliz*, Prague, c. 1748.
- Signed by Maria Theresia and Joseph Graf Harrach. Catalogue record (REPORTED): American Revolution Institute, https://collections.americanrevolutioninstitute.org/record/14713.
- It is c. 1748, not 1740, but it is the nearest official definition of Portion, Ration, Vorspann and remount. Find a digitised copy (ÖNB/ANNO, Google Books or BSB) and read the Verpflegung and Vorspann tariffs.

### 5. What changes for the adjudicator (suggestions, not code)

- **Weather, late Dec 1740:** heavy rain and mud, then frost. Snow possible. Expect the winter of 1740/41 to be cold, consistent with W3–W4. The ice state of the Oder and of the ditches is unknown, so the adjudicator should not assert it and may describe it only as observed by a character.
- **Rations:** about 2 lb of bread a man a day, as a range. Horse rations, wagon loads and bread days carried have no baseline. The adjudicator should describe shortage qualitatively rather than invent weights.
- **Prices:** no 1740 Habsburg price is verified. Dearth and rising prices are attested in general terms. Bohemia exported grain in Oct 1740, so famine was not uniform. Prefer qualitative price talk such as "dear" or "doubled" over invented kr figures.
- **Pay:** 6–8 kr a day for a private (provisional). Officers' pay is unverified.
